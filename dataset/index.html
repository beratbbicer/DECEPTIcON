Dataset#

DECEPTIcON contains clips of fact-checked statements by 100 prominent American public figures, predominantly elected officials, recorded between 2016 and 2021. This page describes what the release contains, how clips were collected and standardised, where labels come from, and what the dataset does not contain. Every value below is from the published work.

Why this dataset#

  • Subject specificity. Every clip comes from one of 100 subjects, all prominent public figures, which holds the speaking setting roughly constant while the speaker varies.
  • Temporal coverage. Collection spans 2016–2021, giving the benchmark a fixed window that later work can match.
  • Label provenance. Every label comes from PolitiFact’s Truth-O-Meter, applied under the outlet’s published rubric, rather than from a rubric written for the dataset.
  • Fixed benchmark split. Evaluation uses a subject-independent 5-fold split that ships with the released setup, so scores from different groups are comparable.
  • Multimodal coverage. Text, audio, and visual representations describe the same statements.

Why PolitiFact annotations#

The labels originate with PolitiFact, a news organisation that checks statements by public figures and publishes each verdict. Each label is the rating issued for a specific statement on that outlet’s Truth-O-Meter under a rubric the outlet writes and publishes: a fixed set of written rules assigning a statement to one level of the scale. This base release adopts those ratings as issued rather than commissioning its own annotation rubric (Table 2).

The criterion is thus external: neither the dataset’s constructors nor the developers of the models scored against it defined accuracy. Uniform application of one documented rubric excludes the variation arising when annotators work from an unwritten shared standard, and rules out an in-house rubric written after the data or results were known.

Rubric and ratings are public: each rating stands beside the outlet’s published fact-check. The ratings predate the dataset, having been assigned as statements were checked, not produced with this benchmark or its models in view. The way a rating is chosen and the review it receives before publication are documented in PolitiFact’s published methodology.

PolitiFact selects which statements to check, so the corpus reflects editorial selection rather than political speech at large; the consequence is recorded under Known limitations. Within the corpus, a label records the fact-checker’s judgement of accuracy, not the speaker’s intent, as Annotations sets out. The claim concerns the provenance of the criterion, not a measurement of what any design choice altered.

Collection process#

Statements were retrieved from PolitiFact. For each statement, the corresponding video was located on a public host — C-SPAN, YouTube, or the Internet Archive — and clipped at the verified timestamp. Demographic attributes were annotated for each subject. Two crops were produced per statement: a short crop and a long crop, giving the same statement at different temporal context (see the citation page for the published description).

Standardisation#

Standardisation means applying the same transformation to every clip, so that differences between clips reflect the speaker and the statement rather than the recording.

Video is normalised to 30 frames per second, faces are tracked with MTCNN, frames are histogram-equalised and normalised with HRN, a 3D face-appearance method, then resized to 256×256 RGB. Audio is resampled to 44.1 kHz. Text has non-ASCII characters removed and contractions expanded (§Data Standardization and Legal Compliance; §Baselines).

Annotations#

Labels come from PolitiFact’s Truth-O-Meter and are assigned under that outlet’s published rubric. A label records the factual accuracy of a statement as the fact-checker judged it. It does not record whether the speaker intended to deceive. A statement rated False may be an error, a misreading, an outdated figure, or a deliberate misrepresentation; the source rubric does not distinguish among these. The dataset adds no intent label, and this site makes no determination about any person (see the citation page for the published description).

The six levels collapse into four evaluation schemes: 6-class, 3-class, binary-NF, and binary-NT. The experimental setup page gives the mappings.

What the dataset does not contain#

  • Media files. The release ships source URLs, timestamps, transcripts, and annotations, not copies of video or audio.
  • Non-political speech. Every clip is a fact-checked statement by a public figure speaking in political life.
  • Non-US subjects. The subject pool is American.

Known limitations#

  • The labels measure factual accuracy, not intent.
  • PolitiFact selects which statements to check, so the corpus over-represents high-profile and controversial claims relative to political speech as a whole.
  • Source quality varies across statements. A label records the fact-checker’s conclusion under its rubric, not a measure of evidence quality.
